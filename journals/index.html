Spaces, Technology, and the Soul of the Web

Journal Reflections on Four Readings

A Rant About “Technology” by Ursula K. Le Guin

This reading made me pause in a way I didn't expect. At first, I thought it was going to be a simple argument about science fiction, but what I found was a bigger truth: we have completely narrowed the meaning of “technology.” Today, when someone says “tech,” we think of phones, AI, apps, VR things that feel shiny, futuristic, and high status. But Le Guin reminds us that technology is not just the new and flashy. It's the oldest and simplest human tools. It's fire. It's a fishhook. It's the way we survive.

That line “Technology is the active human interface with the material world” hit me like a punch to the gut because it's so simple, but so profound. It made me realize how deeply disconnected we are from the things that keep us alive. We live in a culture that glorifies complexity but ignores the quiet brilliance of the basics. A spoon is technology. A shoe is technology. We call them “low-tech,” but if you've ever tried to make one from scratch, you know how complex they are.

This reading also changed how I see her writing. People assume “soft sci-fi” means “less tech,” but her point is that even when stories focus on culture, emotion, and society, they're still deeply technological, because you can't describe a future without describing how people eat, travel, heal, or build. I love that. It made me realize that technology is not just objects, it's culture in action.

I think about my own life, I scroll my phone every day, but I don't know how to make the simplest things. I couldn't make a pair of shoes or start a fire without a lighter. That makes me wonder, have we lost respect for what came before? For the technologies that got us here? This reading reminded me that maybe the most powerful forms of tech are the ones that quietly sustain life, not the ones that grab headlines.

What Is a Website? by Laurel Schwulst

This reading felt like a deep breath, a pause from the chaos of social media to remind me what the web could be. I loved how it framed a website as a space instead of a product. It's such a simple shift, but it changes everything. Suddenly, the web feels less like a feed to scroll through and more like a room to enter, a room I can design to reflect who I am.

The metaphors were my favorite part: a website as a room, a garden, a shelf, even a puddle. They made me imagine what my own “room” on the web would look like. Would it be quiet and warm, filled with sunlight and small objects that tell stories? Or would it be like a wild garden, growing slowly, full of surprises? These metaphors made me feel hopeful because they open up the possibility of making something personal, something that doesn't need to chase likes or go viral.

The author's point about the early web being a commonwealth really stayed with me. The internet was once imagined as a public space, a place for sharing, connection, and creativity. Now, so much of it feels like a shopping mall, built for transactions instead of relationships. That's why this reading feels like an invitation: to take back some of that spirit, even if it's just by making one small personal website.

What I took away from this reading is that a website doesn't have to be finished or perfect. It can be temporary, evolving, maybe even strange. And honestly, that feels like a kind of resistance. It's saying, “I'm not just a user, I'm a maker.” After reading this, I want to create something, not because I need an audience, but because I need a place to breathe.

Handmade Web by J.R. Carpenter

This reading broke something open in me. It felt like both a history lesson and a love letter, to a web that once was and could still be, if we let it. The phrase “handmade web” feels almost radical now. The idea that people once coded pages by hand, made things imperfectly, and shared them without algorithms deciding who should see them, it feels almost mythical compared to the corporate web we live in today.

What struck me most was how the author connected handmade websites to zines, pamphlets, and artist books. That comparison made me feel nostalgic for something I never even fully experienced: a web that was messy, experimental, full of personality. The mid '90s web wasn't sleek, but it was alive. It was “bright, rich, personal, slow, and under construction.” Reading that line made me sad and inspired at the same time, sad because so much of the web now feels standardized and locked down; inspired because nothing is stopping us from making small, weird, personal spaces again.

Another thing that hit me was the idea that creating by hand is a form of resistance. In a world where platforms control almost everything we do online, hand coding even a simple page feels like reclaiming agency. It's saying, “I understand how this works, and I don't need permission to build something of my own.” That feels powerful.

But the reading also had a bittersweet tone, especially the part about how handheld devices have distanced us from that handmade spirit. Phones and tablets make everything smooth and effortless, but at a cost. We don't see the code. We can't peek behind the curtain. We just tap and scroll, passive and dependent. That realization hit hard. It made me want to learn more, to touch the code again, to feel the friction of making something real and mine.

I finished this reading thinking about slowness. About how the handmade web wasn't just a technical process, it was an attitude. It was about curiosity, play, and care. And maybe that's what we've lost most of all: the willingness to go slow, to make something that doesn't scale, to create for joy instead of metrics.

The Good Room by Frank Chimero

This reading touched something deep in me. The line “Spaces speak. Digital ones should, too.” felt like truth. We live so much of our lives in digital spaces now, but do they really speak to us? Or do they just shout at us, demand our attention, and leave us empty? That's what this essay made me ask.

The comparison between the New York Public Library and Penn Station was brilliant. One is a space that lifts you up, the other, a space that wears you down. When the author described the Rose Reading Room, the high ceilings, the quiet beauty, I could feel it. And then he asked: if technology is a place where we live now, what kind of rooms are we building? That question broke me open. Because honestly? Most of the digital rooms I live in feel more like Penn Station, crowded, noisy, designed for transactions, not for people.

The part about the lions, Patience and Fortitude, made me nostalgic for a time when naming things carried meaning. Now, we name apps after speed, trends, or nonsense syllables. What would happen if we designed technology with the same values as a library, spaces that care more about you than your wallet? Spaces that ask nothing from you except to just be?

This reading gave me both clarity and hope. It made me see how over commercialized the web has become, but it also reminded me that this isn't permanent. We can choose different spaces. We can design good rooms, even online. But it requires intention. It requires imagination. And it requires us to believe that technology can have a soul, that it can nourish us, not just monetize us.

When the author wrote about sitting in the library, feeling grateful, I thought about what my own version of that space could be. Maybe it's not a physical room. Maybe it's a quiet corner of the web, a place I build, slowly and with care, where I can just exist without being sold something or measured by metrics. That feels like a dream worth chasing.

Final Reflection

After reading all four of these essays, I feel something I didn't expect: hope. Each reading started with a critique, how we misuse the word technology, how websites became commercial, how the handmade spirit was lost, how our digital spaces feel exhausting. But running through all of them is an invitation: we can do better.

Le Guin reminded me that technology is not just the future, it's the oldest tools, the small things that sustain life. Schwulst showed me that a website can be as personal and expressive as a room, a garden, or a shelf. Carpenter made me want to slow down and make things by hand, to resist the lock-in of platforms and create something weird and mine. And Chimero, he made me dream. Dream of digital spaces that feel like the Rose Reading Room instead of Penn Station. Spaces that nourish instead of drain.

All four readings point to the same truth: technology isn't neutral, and it isn't inevitable. We shape it, and it shapes us. If the web feels commercialized and soulless, maybe that's because we let convenience outrun care. But we can choose slowness. We can choose imagination. We can choose to build good rooms, online and off, that make us feel human again.
